Piet Mondrian arrived in New York in 1940. The energy of Manhattan, its streets, movement, lights and music transformed the way he thought about rhythm.

THE ARTIST

PIET
MONDRIAN

1872–1944

Mondrian was a Dutch painter associated with De Stijl and abstraction. His work reduced visual composition to basic relationships between line, space and color.

RHYTHM

BOOGIE

Mondrian was fascinated by boogie-woogie, a form of jazz driven by repetition, syncopation and rhythm.

In the painting, small blocks of color interrupt the grid like beats moving through a piece of music.

The network of horizontal and vertical lines recalls Manhattan's street grid. Colored squares appear to travel through intersections like taxis, lights and people moving through the city.

A CHANGE

NO
BLACK.

Earlier Mondrian paintings often used strong black lines to separate large areas of primary color.

In Broadway Boogie Woogie, those heavy black divisions disappear. Yellow becomes the dominant structure, broken apart by red, blue, gray and white.

INTERSECTION.

The painting does not contain a literal street, building, car or person.

Instead, Mondrian transforms the sensation of the city into a system of color, repetition and movement.

What happens when a painting becomes an interface?

This website translates Mondrian's visual system into digital space. Information, typography and color occupy the grid instead of being placed outside it.

The painting becomes architecture. The architecture becomes navigation. The information becomes part of the image.
